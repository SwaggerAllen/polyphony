The world bible editor

Four fields, a cover, and a generation affordance on every one of them. The interesting problems here aren't the form — they're what a stranger sees before they commit, and what happens when two campaigns share the same world.

00 · The cover

A blurb, not a subset

The existing fields are written for the Director. Setting and tone are instructions to a model. A person deciding whether to use this world wants different prose entirely, so the cover is its own field rather than a default-visible slice of the others.
Generating it is a good use of the engine. Write a blurb from everything here, including the secrets, that gives none of them away. The model has the whole bible and an explicit instruction not to spoil it.
Anything can be marked secret. One control, three places — a rule, a canon entry, a character's fact. Secrets stay out of the cover, out of browse, and out of any perspective that shouldn't have them.
You can take a world without reading it. Save it off the cover and find out what's in it while you play. For a solo writer who wants to be surprised by their own campaign, that's the whole point.

04 · Rules, canon, secrets

One list component, one secret control

05 · Reuse & visibility

A library world is a template

Attaching a world to a campaign copies it, because a campaign writes its own history onto its world — two campaigns can't accumulate different pasts on one bible. So editing here never reaches a campaign that already started, which removes a whole class of hazard and adds one honest limitation.

06 · Preview as

Authoring is omniscient; previewing is not

The rule that keeps view-as safe outside the play screen: you author omniscient and preview read-only. Editing a bible while looking at a filtered version of it is how someone deletes something they couldn't see.
