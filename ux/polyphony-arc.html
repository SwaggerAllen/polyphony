Polyphony · step 12 · arc review

What play made of them

A scene closes and the engine proposes what changed — about the people in it, and about the world. Nothing is true until you say so. This is also where the sheet stops being one document and becomes a projection you can wind back.

00 · The model

A sheet is a projection, like a scene is

What you wrote is the base; arc is a stack on top. The sheet you read is the two composed together, and it defaults to the latest — because that's who she is now, and it's what every generation is working from.
You can wind it back by scene. Not by beat — arc is extracted when a scene closes, so end-of-scene is the only meaningful resolution, and it matches the contents list a reader sees.
Editing splits two ways, but only where it's ambiguous. A field play hasn't touched edits normally. A field it has revised asks whether you're correcting the original or adding a new change — the same distinction as editing a turn in place versus branching.
A reader's cover is her earliest state in this campaign. Not her earliest ever — someone brought in from a prequel starts here as they ended there. Which makes the spoiler-safe version the automatic one.

03b · When a group changes

One change, fanned out

The template and its members are separate proposals. Groups seed by copy, so updating the template only reaches people written from it later. Current members get their own proposals, through the same path as any other character.
Nothing propagates silently. That's the rule everywhere else and it holds here — six members means six things you can say yes or no to, not one switch that rewrites six sheets.
Which makes dissent free. Accept the group's change, reject one member's, and you've written the person who didn't go along with it. That's a story beat you'd otherwise have to author by hand.
Off-screen members are included. Same as a world fact — the group changed and they're in it, so they get the proposal whether or not they were in the scene.

04c · Fixing it where you are

The row tells you, and the row fixes it

On the row, not at the bottom. You find out Wren is behind the moment you add her, not after you've written a premise and picked four other people. A gate discovered at the submit button is a gate that feels like a trap.
Review happens in place. Expanding the row shows the actual proposals, with the same accept and edit controls as the review screen. Nobody gets sent to another page holding a half-built scene.
One tap is always available. Accept-all sits on the row itself. The person who wants to read gets the expansion; the person who wants to play gets a single button, and both end up consistent.
Same rows everywhere you cast. Scene setup, adding someone mid-scene, and admitting a Director proposal are the same component — so the fix is in the same place every time you meet it.
