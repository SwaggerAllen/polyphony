Polyphony · step 11 · browse & published

Reading someone else's

Publishing works today and produces nothing anyone can read — Browse is bare title cards. This is the missing half: a reading surface where the perspective picker is the whole draw, because no other reading app can offer the same scene from three heads.

00 · What publishing decides

Two separate questions, not one ladder

How it's meant to be read is a content decision — which perspectives a reader may take. Spectator isn't a lesser tier, it's one of the options, and a publisher can leave it out if the story doesn't work without someone's head.
Whether the authoring surface is exposed is a permission decision, and it's a single checkbox: forkable. Sheets come with it, because a fork has to be able to carry the story on and can't do that from prose alone.
How it's read · pick any
Spectator

What a camera caught. Neutral, and the default only because it's the one option that reveals nothing — not because it's the best read.

How it's read
Everyone you've shared

Every published perspective at once, and nothing beyond them. This is how novels are written — prose without interiority is a screenplay, and most people don't want to read one.

How it's read
One person at a time

Pick who a reader may inhabit. The spoiler control: the one secretly working against the rest stays shut unless you open her.

Permission · one checkbox
Forkable

Someone can take the whole thing and carry it on — world, cast, sheets, arc. Independent of how you let people read it.

Everyone-you've-shared is the one to build first. It's the mode a reader who just wants the story will pick, it needs no choice from them, and it's the only one that reads like fiction rather than a record. It gets a character's visual treatment rather than the author's omniscient view — it isn't an authoring surface, it's a way of reading.

03 · The reason to build this

The same scene, twice

One beat, two readers. Halden doesn't know what Ruthe already decided, and Ruthe doesn't hear what he says to the man on the stair. Neither version is marked up or annotated — each just reads as a scene, and the reader who switches finds out what they were missing. This is the play screen with a different bottom bar — same header, same perspective control, same transcript, same beat rules. Only the composer is replaced, by scene navigation.

04 · Taking something with you

Fork, or just take the world

Three different appetites: carry on this story, borrow the setting, or keep the person. All three are copies into your library, and all three say so plainly.

Fork · forkable
Carry this on yourself?

You'll get your own copy of everything — the world, all seven people, and everything that happened to them. It picks up where this leaves off, and nothing you do touches the original.

Ilse's version stays exactly as it is. Yours becomes a different story from the first thing you change.

04b · Copies everywhere

Group by what it came from

Every campaign copies its world, every fork copies everything, so within a year there are a dozen Saltmarches. Flat lists of near-identical names are unusable — so both Browse and your library group by the original and let you open the version you actually meant. In your library that's by campaign; in Browse it's by author.

05 · Reporting, and the edges

One report path, reachable from every published thing

The moderation queue is fully built and has never had a way in. Report targets the frozen snapshot, but a take-down reaches the author's own campaign too — hiding only the listing would leave them free to publish it again. It's distinct from flagging a bad generation in your own scene, which goes nowhere near a human.
