Polyphony · step 6 · campaign editor

The campaign is the hub

One create entry point, five panels, and an ordered flow that gets out of the way once you've been through it. Every panel empty and populated. Stage register throughout — authoring is working, so the reading register never appears here.

00 · Two changes to the brief's tab order

Settings · World · Cast · Groups · Premise · Scenes

Quick Build isn't a tab. It's a one-shot — you use it once, on an empty campaign, and never again. A permanent tab is dead weight from day two and invites running it over a campaign you've already built. It's a card in the first-run state.
Scenes takes the slot. The campaign is the hub, so it has to be where you go to play. It's also where arc review surfaces, which fixes an orphan route the catalog flags.
Cast comes before Premise. Least-specific-to-most is the intuitive order, but generation order wins: the pitch is written from the cast, so writing it first means writing it twice.
Nothing is ever removed, only archived. Characters are referenced by every transcript they appear in. Removing one would invalidate history, so the destructive-sounding control is Archive, everywhere.
Groups sit beside Cast. They're written with the same editor and they seed characters, so they belong next to the people they produce rather than buried in a menu.
A campaign can be finished. Not archived — finished, deliberately, which is what makes it eligible to be another campaign's prequel and what turns the Scenes tab into a record rather than a workspace.

07 · Scenes

Readable at a glance, no generated titles

The location is the title — you authored it, it's always there, and it doesn't cost a generation or risk being wrong. Under it: the premise for a scene that hasn't closed, the omniscient summary once it has. Both always visible, not collapsed. On a long campaign this reads like a table of contents, and it degrades correctly — summaries don't exist until scene-close is wired, so premise is the fallback that always works.

08 · Long lists

Render everything, but structure it

Punt, deliberately. Realistic ceilings are small — a hundred characters, fifty scenes, a few hundred library entries. Server-rendered rows at that scale are nothing, and paginating them now costs you effort and costs the user a click for a problem they don't have.
Tiering and grouping are the real answer. The list that actually gets long is walk-ons, and it's already collapsed behind a count. Structure beats pagination — a hundred rows in four labelled groups reads fine; a hundred flat rows don't, paginated or not.
If a group does get long, add search before paging. People looking for a specific character type a name; they don't page through looking for it. Search plus tier filters covers nearly every case pagination would have.
When it's finally needed, "Load more" — not infinite scroll. Infinite scroll fights back-navigation, and returning from a character sheet to find you've lost your place is worse than a button. It's also far simpler over LiveView, which streams appended rows natively.

One thing worth doing now, since it's free: build every list row so it doesn't depend on its neighbours. Then adding a window later is a change to the query, not to the markup.

10 · The branch selector

The hub shows one line, and says which

Choosing a branch scopes the whole hub to it — its cast, its groups, its world, its scenes. Copy-on-branch means two lines hold different Wrens, and the design that never has to explain which Wren you're looking at is one that never shows both. The cost is a mode, so the header has to name it. Same pill as play's, because it is the same control. Here it shares its row with the perspective selector; privacy and the menu move up beside the title, which thins a top row that was carrying four things.

10b · The navigator

Full screen, because the people who open it have a lot to look at

Bimodal by expectation: most campaigns never branch, and the ones that do branch a great deal. A dropdown would serve the case that doesn't exist. Opens unfolded to where you are, with the current line marked. Each row carries when it was made — recall runs on the one from Tuesday evening far more reliably than on any generated name.
